Three languages and the journey of a poem

Odia, Hindi and English share a home in Kabita Live. Between them, a poem can find another reader while keeping the voice, place and feeling that make it its own.

A poem begins in a particular language. Its tenderness may live in a form of address, its humor in the distance between a question and an answer, its grief in something the speaker leaves unsaid. A translation has to notice these things before it can carry them elsewhere.

A home in Odia, Hindi and English

Kabita Live is rooted in Odia and focuses on poetry in Odia, Hindi and English. These three languages give the magazine a clear field of attention: poems written in each language, and translations that let readers encounter work they might otherwise miss.

Each language has room here as a language of original writing. An English poem need not begin in Odia; a Hindi poem has its own voice and setting. Translation can move between any of the three. The original remains the point of reference, with the poet and any credited translator named alongside the work.

A reader may feel at home in one language and curious about another. Someone who reads two or all three may notice how a phrase changes its weight, where a pause moves, or what a translation leaves unresolved. The magazine can make space for both ways of reading.

What should travel with the poem?

Consider an Odia saying: ଠାକୁର ଘରେ କିଏ — ମୁଁ କଦଳୀ ଖାଇନାହିଁ। Literally: “Who’s in the prayer room?”—“I didn’t eat the bananas!” Nobody asked about bananas. The unprompted denial gives the speaker away.

An English adaptation might say, “Who’s in the kitchen?”—“I didn’t eat the cookies!” The setting changes, but the listener recognizes the same human behavior. That version is an adaptation, not a word-for-word translation. In a literary work, keeping the prayer room and bananas may be the better choice: the original setting can carry a significance that should not disappear merely because it is unfamiliar.

The same question matters when a poem moves between Odia and Hindi, Hindi and English, or English and Odia. What carries the feeling: the literal object, the sound of the line, a familiar expression, or the way one person addresses another? The answer belongs to the particular poem.

The reader must recognize a living poem

A translated poem needs its meaning, movement and emotional pressure. A joke should give the reader room to recognize the joke. A hesitant admission should remain hesitant. A plain sentence should not become ornate simply because it has crossed a language boundary.

Natural translation also needs judgment about cultural detail. A reference can be retained, briefly explained or adapted, depending on its role in the poem. Familiarity is not always the goal. A reader can enter an unfamiliar world if the translation gives enough footing without explaining away its mystery.

Care also means leaving a question open when the source is uncertain. A form of address may carry intimacy or respect; a word may hold more than one meaning. A fluent line is not a reason to invent a relationship, settle an ambiguity or change who is acting.

On Kabita Live, readers can return to the original and choose an available Odia, Hindi or English version. Additional versions prepared with AI assistance are labelled as draft interpretations. Independent linguistic review is still pending. A missing version should remain visibly unavailable until there is a translation to read.

What people teach AI about a language

A personal reflection by Ahimanikya Satapathy

I think of AI as an intern in a company: capable, quick to contribute, and still in need of guidance. It makes mistakes. A confident answer can miss something that a person with lived experience notices immediately. Yet I believe that patient feedback can make AI a powerful creative partner, with possibilities beyond what we can imagine today.

Language is more than the rules written in a grammar book. It carries a community’s culture: how people express affection, tease one another, show respect, grieve, protest and leave things unsaid. A sentence can be grammatically correct and still feel wrong to the people whose language it is. Helping AI recognize that difference takes human experience and judgment.

For Kabita Live, that attention belongs to Odia, Hindi and English. When I say I am teaching the AI I work with, I mean explaining cultural context, questioning its choices, comparing versions and keeping the lessons from each correction in the translation process. Each revision is an opportunity to carry the poem’s meaning, emotional force and voice more faithfully.

My knowledge of Odia is one part of that work. Readers who live in Hindi and English bring knowledge I cannot supply alone. Their feedback matters: where a phrase feels unnatural, where an image loses its meaning, where the poem no longer sounds like a person speaking. That collaboration is where I see AI becoming a powerful creative partner.

Three languages offer three ways into the magazine. The work is to listen carefully to each poem, and to the people who read it.
