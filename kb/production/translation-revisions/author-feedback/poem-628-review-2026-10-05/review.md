# Shakuntala Gupta: translation review

**ମୋ ଭିତରେ ଗାଆଁଟିଏ · A Village Within Me · Edition 35**  
5 October 2026 · Hindi and English · AI self-review using Human Natural Translation / Poetic Natural.

Read the complete saved Odia and both current translations. The poem’s major images and emotional movement survive: the invitation into the village, its tactile everyday life, the separation of hearts, remembered intimacy, the village as bride/beloved, and the speaker’s absence within the closing invitation. Keep the uncertainty of that absence: neither death nor loss of ego is explicitly established as the only reading.

The most useful revision is focused. Preserve the poem’s imagery and unusual associations while improving gender choices and a few literal constructions. **Update: Ahimanikya authorized applying the targeted fixes after reviewing the PDF.** The Hindi village references and central stanza, three Hindi phrasing repairs, English personification and lane/bend phrase are now applied locally in batch-022. The source is unchanged and author refinement remains pending. The review and original snapshots below preserve the earlier proposal; they do not claim the poet approved the final wording. Full snapshots and hashes are in [review.json](review.json).

|---|---|---|

Do not infer either participant’s gender from the feminine village. |
| English village: It … itself | Grammatical, but less explicitly personified than She … herself. | A strong candidate in the bride/beloved stanza, supported by the reported intention. This English wording has not been approved by the poet. |

| Tree/name/leaf line | English “Each tree … its own name” resolves whose name is meant. The source’s compressed saying leaves the relation less clear to this reviewer. | Ask for the intended sense; do not silently create a proverb or declare a guessed explanation correct. |

## Central stanza: proposed revision

### Hindi candidate

मेरे न होने पर,  
मेरी टोली ऐसे खिलखिलाती है,  
नई बहू की तरह।  
घाव और अभाव को गहना बनाकर पहनती है,  
सजती है,  
ईश्वर की गुप्त प्रेयसी की तरह।  

This follows Shakuntala’s suggested **टोली** and changes the village’s agreement. “मेरे न होने पर” avoids separately assuming the speaker is female. “नई बहू” and the wounds/want-as-ornaments image remain. This is a stanza candidate, not permission to mix गाँव and टोली arbitrarily through the full poem.

If adopting टोली throughout, review the title **मेरे भीतर एक टोली**, the map reference, the mahua/kadamba passage, the invitation and closing description. Hindi agreement still follows the actual construction: **मेरी टोली का मानचित्र** retains **का** because मानचित्र is masculine. Do not mechanically change every masculine ending.

### English candidate

When I am not there,  
my village laughs like this, bright and bubbling,  
like a new bride.  
She wears wounds and want as ornaments,  
adorns herself  
like God’s secret beloved.  

Only the personification is changed here. “Wounds and want” is worth retaining: its sound supports the existing image without adding a new one. Do not replace the intimate “secret beloved” with a general religious explanation.

## Small refinements worth testing aloud

| Current wording | Candidate | Reason / limit |
|---|---|---|
| नई दीवारें उठे हुए द्वार। | द्वारों पर नई उठी दीवारें। | Repairs the Hindi construction while keeping walls and doorways. Exact spatial relation is inferred from the source’s compressed line; do not invent who built them. |
| पुरानी स्मृति अब पानी खाई लकड़ी है। | पुरानी स्मृति अब पानी से गली लकड़ी है। | More natural Hindi for water-damaged wood; shifts the image toward decay. Compare with the poet’s intended force before selecting. Keep the English “water-eaten wood” as a possible deliberate, vivid strangeness. |
| सर्दियों का पत्ते झरना कितना सुंदर! | सर्दियों में पत्तों का झरना कितना सुंदर! | Corrects the awkward Hindi construction without relocating the season or changing the image. |
| Whichever lane or bend you pass, | Whichever lane you take, whichever bend you turn, | More idiomatic English; retain the invitation’s direct address. |
| she too will take her fill of seeing you; | she too will feast her eyes on you; | Possible idiomatic rendering of looking to one’s fill. More expressive than the literal version, so compare its tone rather than assume it is superior. |

Avoid polishing everything at once. The unusual granary, divided measure, water-eaten memory and leaf imagery may be doing deliberate poetic work.

## Full stanza-by-stanza assessment

| Stanza | What survives / what needs review |
|---|---|
| 1 — water and map | The stream washing the visitor’s feet and the chalk-written name are present. Both targets end with an awkward “filled … here and there”; clarify its connection to the map/name without adding messages or locations. |
| 2 — tree, red flag, Budhi Maa | The setting is preserved. English keeps Budhi Maa identifiable; Hindi बूढ़ी माँ could read as an ordinary aged mother. The flag and vermilion-covered stone suggest a sacred referent, but the poem’s exact identification needs the poet’s context. Do not invent a named temple or locality. The addressee’s Hindi gender is also provisional. |
| 3 — fields, courtyard, cowshed, granary | Keep the concrete rural material and smell; do not sanitize it into scenic nature. “The pleas of my life” is literal and unusual, but the source’s ହାରିଗୁହାରି compound needs contextual care before smoothing it into an abstract sentiment. |
| 4 — carts and division | The carts and new walls remain. गଉଣି is a cultural measuring term; “grain-measures” is a plausible current reading, not a confirmed explanation of the division. Do not assert inheritance, caste or family conflict absent confirmation. |
| 5 — hearts apart | Both targets retain the question about breaking separation. Preserve its questioning force; removing gender by changing it to an instruction would change the speech act. |
| 6 — old door and water-eaten memory | Preserve the double door, joined hands and material memory. Do not “correct” the physical arrangement into a different scene merely because it is poetic. Improve Hindi syntax selectively. |
| 7 — village as bride and beloved | Highest-priority revision: author feedback supports feminine village personification. Keep wounds and want as adornment; do not romanticize away either. |
| 8 — tree/name/leaf and lifted feet | The tree/name/leaf relation needs clarification. The lifted-feet idiom and sudden turn toward the addressee work in both targets. |
| 9 — mahua, kadamba, winter, dry leaf | Preserve trees, season and the leaf’s touch. Hindi’s winter construction needs repair. “छू जाऊँगी” assumes a female speaker; neutralizing it must not change the promised touch into a wish. |
| 10 — invitation and the absent “I” | Retain the marked “I”, invitation and wild/hilly form. Neither target should decide that absence exclusively means death, spiritual release or simple travel. Review the Hindi noun/ending if टोली is selected. |

## Questions for the poet’s next review

1. In **ଯେ ଯା ଗଛକୁ ତା ନାଁର ପତର**, whose name belongs to the leaf, and is this a local saying or a deliberately individual image?
2. In the **ଗଉଣି** line, what exactly is being divided? The current translations assume grain measures, which needs contextual confirmation.
3. Are the speaker and the addressed “you” intended to have specified genders, or should Hindi preserve their openness?

These are literary clarification questions, not a reason to withdraw the poem. No message has been sent to the poet.

### English — current saved draft

**A Village Within Me**

A sandy channel with a thin ribbon of water  
will wash your feet.  
Then read the map of my village:  
your name scrawled in chalk,  
filled in here and there.  

The red flag beneath the peepal tree,  
the vermilion-smeared stone—that is my Budhi Maa.  
Whichever lane or bend you pass,  
she too will take her fill of seeing you;  
she too sits watching for your arrival.  

Fields, homesteads, dung pits,  
a dung-plastered courtyard,  
the smell of the cowshed—this is my granary,  
my pride and the pleas of my life.  

Along a sandy footpath,  
a couple of bullock carts still travel.  
In some homes the grain-measures have been divided,  
doorways with newly raised walls.  

Can you break down what separates  
one heart from another!?  

You will see the carved, crumbling,  
oldest double-leaved door;  
we stand on its two sides, holding hands.  
Old memory is now water-eaten wood.  

When I am not there,  
my village laughs like this, bright and bubbling,  
like a new bride.  
It wears wounds and want as ornaments,  
adorns itself  
like God's secret beloved.  

Each tree has a leaf bearing its own name!  
Oh, just thinking of you,  
my feet no longer touch the ground!  

Beneath my village's mahua and kadamba,  
how lovely winter's falling leaves!  
I will touch you as a dry leaf.  

Come to my village someday,  
when the ‘I’ is not there!!  
Within me, this village  
is shaped of wilderness and hills.
